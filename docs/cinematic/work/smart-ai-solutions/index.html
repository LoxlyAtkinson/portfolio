The situation

This one had no client and no brief. It had to do two jobs at once: be the public shopfront people find and read, and be the place enquiries, clients, audit runs and reports are handled day to day. It is also the piece of work I point at when someone asks whether I ship, so the verification bar I use on client work had to apply to my own.

What I built

Roughly 390 public pages that are built, not hand-made

The public side carries service, pricing, industry, comparison and location pages, a programmatic set keyed by service and by city, a blog with an RSS feed, and a machine-readable llms.txt layer for AI assistants. All of it is prerendered to static HTML before upload, so a visitor gets real markup rather than an empty shell waiting on JavaScript. The sitemap holds 394 entries and the last recorded deploy prerendered 393 of 393 pages with 0 failures.

The back office behind the brochure

Behind a login sits the part that does the work: a CRM with editable lead tiers, a client registry, GEO audit runs, scheduled reports and live chat, all under a six-group navigation covering Insight, Pipeline, Delivery, Content, Money and System. It is the same codebase and the same deploy as the public site, so there is no second system to keep in step. That navigation was confirmed rendering on production, not just in local development.

Booking, and the self-serve tools in front of it

Availability, creating a booking, cancelling one, letting someone manage their own, and the reminders that follow are all server-side functions inside the same system. Alongside them the public side runs an AI readiness assessment, a lead-magnet library and a voice agent, so a visitor can get something useful out of the site before they ever speak to me.

Reporting and site audits

The back end generates and sends client reports, pulls analytics, takes backlink snapshots, probes citations, scans websites, publishes blog posts, processes sequences, takes CRM lead submissions and assembles an operator digest. Those run as edge functions on 70 Postgres migrations, in the same repository as the public pages rather than in a separate reporting tool.

A build chain that refuses to ship broken work

Building the site runs blog SEO validation, RSS generation, the sitemap, the llms.txt files, an AI-visibility verifier, asset copy and the prerender pass, in that order, so the checks are part of the build rather than something to remember afterwards. There is also a lint step whose only job is to catch em dashes, because house style should be enforced by a machine, not by memory.

Every deploy leaves a receipt

The proofs directory holds more than 40 named proof folders, one per piece of work, each recording what was expected and what was actually observed. The 27 August 2026 record shows 393 of 393 pages prerendered with 0 failed, six production routes returning 200 after upload, and a check that each newly shipped string is present in the uploaded bundle. The proofs are written for the version of me who comes back in three months and needs to know whether something was really verified or merely assumed.

The hard part

Refusing to call a served chunk a rendered screen

The failure that actually hurts on a deploy is not a 500. It is an upload that looks fine and quietly carries a stale build, so the new controls never reach the screen while every route still answers 200. So for the 7 September 2026 deploy I wrote the nine expectations, including what would count as a failure, before taking a single screenshot. Mid-run the login session I was testing with expired, so four of the nine could not be looked at. I could still show that the new JavaScript was being served from production, and that proves the deploy carried the work rather than proving the screens render it, so I wrote that distinction into the file, marked those four items failed, and scored the pass 5 of 9. Once the session was working again, all four were re-run against production and the checklist closed at 9 of 9. The same file records a false negative I caused myself, a PowerShell regex-escape bug that made five strings that were present report as absent, written down rather than quietly fixed. The order is the whole point: author the expectations first, then take the picture, then mark the picture against the list, because a checklist written after the screenshot will always pass.
